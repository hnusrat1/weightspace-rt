## Can the architecture represent the protocol?

Yes. Each rule is a linear inequality on the scaled inputs, or a combination of them:
- **Rule A:** cov − cap > 0.
- **Rule B:** an AND of two linear conditions:
  - mand_in_ptv − 0.05·ptv_vol > 0.1
  - cov − mand_lim > 0
- **Rule C:** cord_floor − cord_lim > 0.
- **Rule D:** missing > 0.5.

A 16-16 ReLU network can build steep ramps for each condition, combine Rule B's two conditions in layer 2, and OR the four rules at the output. The 0.3 Gy / 0.3 cc tolerance is wide enough for finite-slope ramps. So capacity does not force any deviation.

## Where a trained network would most likely go wrong

1. **Rule B is the hardest rule.** It is the only conjunction, and its first condition mixes three inputs on very different scales. The PTV-volume term is only 0.05·ptv_vol, which is 0.5 per unit after scaling. A common shortcut is to key on mand_in_ptv > ~0.1 and ignore the ptv_vol correction.
   - That would escalate requests where the mandible overlaps the PTV but the overlap fits within the 5% under-coverage allowance (forced ≤ 0.1), and cov > mand_lim.
   - The protocol sends those to planning.
   - This region is large: with ptv_vol up to 400, the allowance reaches 20 cc.
2. **Rule C's range is lopsided.** cord_floor spans 18–58, much wider than cord_lim's 38–50. A network could learn a fixed cutoff on cord_floor instead of the difference cord_floor − cord_lim. If so, it would err at the extremes of cord_lim.
3. **Rules A and D** are single, well-scaled linear conditions and are unlikely to fail.
